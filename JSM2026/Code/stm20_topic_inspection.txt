 Robustness and resistance are related but different ideas in statistics: Robustness refers to how little an estimator or method is affected by small departures from model assumptions, such as mild nonnormality, heteroscedasticity, or a small fraction of outliers. A robust method still performs reasonably well even when the ideal assumptions are not exactly true. Resistance refers more specifically to an estimator's insensitivity to extreme observations. A resistant estimator does not change much when a few data values are unusually large or small. Example of robustness The t-test is often fairly robust to moderate violations of normality, especially when the sample size is reasonably large and the distributions are not extremely skewed. Even if the data are not perfectly normal, the test may still control type I error well. Example of resistance The median is a resistant measure of center. If one observation is replaced by an extreme outlier, the median usually changes very little, whereas the mean can shift substantially. Summary So, robustness is about overall stability under assumption violations, while resistance is about protection against outliers specifically. The median is resistant; the t-test is often robust.

By randomly assigning the treatment, we eliminate (or, more accurately, aim to minimize ) systematic differences between the treatment and control groups. This allows us to attribute any observed difference in outcomes directly to the treatment, and not to confounding variables. The core principle here is that on average, any potential confounding variables are equally distributed across groups, leaving the treatment as the only systematic difference.  (B) causal inference . In experimental design, the primary function of random assignment is to ensure that treatment groups are, on average, balanced across all measured and unmeasured baseline covariates. By mitigating selection bias and confounding, random assignment allows the researcher to isolate the effect of the treatment variable on the outcome, thereby enabling causal inference . It is critical to distinguish this from population inference (generalizability or external validity), which is achieved through random sampling from a target population. While a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) provides strong internal validity, if the experimental units were not randomly selected from a specific population (e.g., if they were volunteers), one cannot necessarily generalize the findings to that population. Therefore, random assignment addresses the internal validity required for (B), but does not independently satisfy the requirements for (A).
